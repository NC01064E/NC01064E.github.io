The Assembly Line Comes for Knowledge Work

By Nick Cerbone · June 6, 2026 · cerbone.me

Personal views only. The opinions and thoughts expressed in this blog are my own and do not represent CivicOS Institute, FLSportsDesk.com, or any other employer, past or present.

Knowledge workers are meeting the artisans' moment, and abundance has two sides.

On knowledge work, abundance, and what the Industrial Revolution actually taught us

There is a fear moving through the professions right now, and it has a familiar shape.

The mathematicians feel it when a machine produces in an afternoon what a career was supposed to produce in a decade. The lawyers feel it when a document that once required a team of associates arrives in minutes. Teachers feel it. Writers feel it. Programmers feel it. The fear is rarely stated plainly, because the people feeling it are articulate, and articulate people are good at dressing anxiety up as principle. But underneath the principles is a simpler sentence: the thing I spent my life learning to do is becoming cheap.

We have been here before. We just were not the ones standing in the workshop.

When mechanized production came for the artisans, the artisans were not wrong about what was happening to them. A master craftsman had spent ten years learning to make by hand what a factory could suddenly make by the thousand. His skill was real. His pride in it was earned. And the market for his skill collapsed anyway, because the factory did not have to match his skill. It had to match his output, at a price ordinary people could pay.

History tends to remember the disruption and forget the distribution.

Mass production is usually told as a story about what workers lost. It should also be told as a story about what the poor gained. Before the factory, most manufactured goods were luxuries in the plain sense: a good pair of shoes, a warm coat, a set of tools, a printed book, these were things a working family saved for, inherited, or did without. The industrial shift changed who goods were for. It put products that had been barely affordable within reach of people who had never been able to afford them. That is not a small thing. For the family at the bottom of the economy, it was nearly everything.

The craftsmen paid for that change, and we should not pretend otherwise. Every abundance has a dislocation inside it, and the dislocation lands on real people with real rents and real children. Honesty about the cost is owed. But honesty about the benefit is owed too, and our current conversation about artificial intelligence is mostly missing it.

Because the pattern is repeating, one level up.

For two centuries, the machine took the work of the hand and made it abundant. Now the machine is coming for the work of the mind, and the knowledge workers are discovering what the artisans discovered: scarcity was the business model. The analysis was expensive because analysts were scarce. The brief was expensive because trained writers were scarce. The diagnosis, the lesson plan, the code review, the translation, all of it priced by the rarity of the person who could produce it.

AI does not merely make these things faster. It makes them common. And common is the one thing a scarcity economy does not know how to forgive.

I understand the fear. I have worked in classrooms where the entire institution is organized around the assumption that knowledge is scarce and must be rationed out by credentialed people in scheduled rooms. Some of that assumption was always a polite fiction. More of it becomes fiction every month.

But I keep returning to the question the artisans' critics never quite answered: abundant for whom?

If a family that could never afford a tutor can now sit at the kitchen table and get patient, competent help with algebra at nine o'clock at night, that is not a catastrophe. If a small business that could never afford counsel can now read its own contracts with understanding, that is not a catastrophe. If a resident who could never hire a researcher can now make sense of her city's budget, the city is better for it. The people who experience knowledge abundance as a threat are mostly the people who were selling the scarcity. The people who experience it as a gift are the ones who were paying.

That was true at the factory gate. It is true at the prompt window.

None of this means the transition will be kind. The industrial one was not kind, and it took generations of law, labor, and plain stubbornness to distribute its gains decently. We will have our own version of that work to do, and it will not do itself.

But the direction of a technology is not judged only by what it does to the guild. It is also judged by what it does to the household.

The guild will survive in some form. It always does, smaller, stranger, more focused on the parts of the work that were never really about output. The household will be fine too, if we let the abundance actually reach it instead of fencing it back into scarcity for the comfort of the people who used to sell tickets to it.

The artisans were not fools for fearing the factory. We would be fools for learning nothing from what the factory gave everyone else.
