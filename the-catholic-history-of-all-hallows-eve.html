The Catholic History of All Hallows' Eve

By Nick Cerbone · October 31, 2026 · cerbone.me

Personal views only. The opinions and thoughts expressed in this blog are my own and do not represent CivicOS Institute, FLSportsDesk.com, or any other employer, past or present.

Halloween is named for the saints, and the documents say the Church built the night. France supplied the costumes, Ireland the Eve, and America made a stew of the whole inheritance.

On saints, soul cakes, and the night the Church taught us to laugh at death

The strangest thing about Halloween is its name. It gives itself away. A hallow is a holy person, a saint, the same word we pray in hallowed be thy name. All Hallows' Eve is simply the evening before the feast of All Saints, worn down by centuries of tongues into a word most people could not parse. Whatever got piled onto the night later, the sign over the door was always ours. The night is named, in plain English, after the saints.

It began with the martyrs. The early Church kept the anniversary of a martyr's death at the place of the martyrdom, and treated that day as a birthday. Then the Diocletian persecution produced more martyrs than the calendar could hold one by one, and the Church, unwilling to let any of them go unremembered, did the practical and beautiful thing: it appointed a common day for them all.

Rome gave that day its shape. In 609, Pope Boniface IV took the Pantheon, the great temple built to all the gods, and consecrated it to the Blessed Virgin and all the martyrs, the house of every god handed over to the men and women who had died rather than burn a pinch of incense to any of them. The feast sat on May 13 at first. A century later Gregory III dedicated a chapel in St. Peter's to all the saints and fixed its anniversary on November 1, and in 837 Gregory IV extended the November date to the whole Church. The vigil, the night of watching before the feast, was kept from the earliest days, as vigils always are. Nobody schedules the night before a great day unless the day matters.

And Samhain? Yes, the Irish marked their own festival of the dead season at the same hinge of the year, and no honest history should pretend otherwise. But the tidy story told every October, that Rome moved the feast to November 1 to supplant Samhain, runs well ahead of the documents. The documents show a Roman feast with Roman reasons: a chapel dedication, a pope's calendar, and a Church that needed no help from druids to honor its dead. What actually happened is humbler and more familiar. Wherever the faith landed, local habits braided themselves around the feast, the way ivy braids around a wall. The Church has rarely been afraid of the ivy. She insists on the wall.

The third day completes the architecture. In 998, Odilo, abbot of Cluny, ordered that the day after All Saints be kept in all his monasteries for the faithful departed, every one of them, and Rome adopted it for the whole Church. Praying for the dead was no medieval invention: Judas Maccabeus was already making offerings for his fallen soldiers two centuries before Christ, and the catacombs are scratched with requests for the same prayers. So the triduum stands: the Eve, the Saints, the Souls. It is the communion of saints drawn on a calendar. The Church in glory, the Church being purified, the Church still on the road, one family in three days, and the veil between them treated as a curtain in a house rather than a wall between strangers.

The customs carried all of this door to door. On the Eve and on All Souls, children and the poor went souling: they knocked, they offered to pray for the household's dead by name, and in exchange they received soul cakes, apples, small coins, ale in some places for the grown. One for Peter, two for Paul, three for Him who made us all. By 1380 the preacher John Mirk was already describing souling as an old custom: good men and women, he said, gave the bread out hoping that with each loaf they might get a soul out of purgatory. Charity and intercession, baked into one small round cake and traded at the door. The dead were prayed for, the poor were fed, the children were sugared, and everybody in the transaction understood what it was for.

The grotesque has a French address. In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the Black Death cut Europe in half, and France answered on its cemetery walls with the danse macabre, the dance of death: corpses leading popes and kings, knights and monks, peasants and children, all by the hand to the same grave. On All Souls the picture sometimes stepped down off the wall and was staged as a living tableau, men and women dressed as the dead and as every estate of life, processing in the flesh what the painters had put in paint. That is where the dressing up starts. Not on the Eve, and not in Ireland. In France, on the day of the souls, in a country that had buried half its people and was determined to look at death standing up.

The first meaning of the dance was a reminder: the grave takes every rank alike. But the Church has never been able to leave death merely solemn, because her creed will not permit it. Death, for a people who believe it has been beaten, is a tyrant deposed, and deposed tyrants get laughed at in the public square. That reading worked its way into the custom itself. A people taught for centuries to mock death will in the end dress as death and its servants: the corpse, the skeleton, and eventually the devil himself, worn lightly by children who are not afraid of him. O death, where is thy sting? The rubber skeletons and plastic gravestones on the lawn are the folk remnant of that contempt. We dress up as the thing that lost.

One more date. On this Eve in 1517, an Augustinian monk named Martin Luther posted his theses in Wittenberg, and he chose the day with care: the town would be packed for All Saints, its castle church famous for one of the great relic collections of Europe. The Reformation fired its opening shot on the Church's own feast day, counting on the Church's own crowds. Whatever else divides Christians five centuries later, the calendar that week was Catholic enough to fill a town, and the argument was about the very thing All Souls exists for: what the living owe the dead.

Then the whole inheritance crossed the ocean, in pieces, and America made a stew of it. The strings arrived separately and kept to their own neighborhoods for a long time. France brought the costuming, born in its All Souls tableaux. Ireland brought the Eve itself: the night watch, souling at the door, lanterns carved from turnips before America offered the pumpkin. Scotland brought guising, the disguise for going out into the night. England brought masked mischief of its own from the far side of the calendar. In the colonies of the 1700s, Irish and French Catholics began marrying each other, and their customs married too. The nineteenth century poured in the rest, wave after wave of immigrants carrying a parish's worth of autumn in their luggage, until the pieces finally cooked down into one holiday. The twentieth century canned it: the words trick or treat in print in Canada by the late 1920s, and after the war the suburbs serving the same dish on every street in the country.

American trick or treat is souling's grandchild, and like a grandchild it kept the shape and lost the catechism. The knocking survived, the food survived, the disguises survived. The prayers fell off somewhere in the crossing, until nothing was left in the bag but candy and the threat of a prank. I do not say this to scold the grandchild. I say it because the inheritance is still there under the plastic, waiting to be claimed by anyone who knows the family name.

So when I hear Christians today debating whether to cede Halloween to the darkness, I want to hand them the documents. The night was never the devil's. It is the vigil of the saints, built by the Church, and the only surrender on offer is of something that was ours to begin with. A vigil is not a party and it is not a panic. It is a night of watching kept by people who know what tomorrow celebrates: that the dead in Christ are not gone, that the family is larger than the visible part of it, and that death itself has been made a fool of in public. Hand out the candy. Carve the pumpkin. When the doorbell rings, say a prayer for your own dead while the children are reaching into the bowl. That is not a compromise with the culture. That is the original practice, soul cakes or no soul cakes. The Eve was always a vigil, and a vigil is what you keep when you expect the morning.
