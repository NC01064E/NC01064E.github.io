When Answers Are Free

By Nick Cerbone · July 10, 2026 · cerbone.me

Personal views only. The opinions and thoughts expressed in this blog are my own and do not represent CivicOS Institute, FLSportsDesk.com, or any other employer, past or present.

Schools were built to ration scarce information. The scarcity ended. The formation remains.

On school, formation, and what a teacher is actually for

Here is a question every school in America is quietly asking and almost none are saying out loud: if a student can get a competent answer to almost any question, instantly, for free, at any hour — what exactly is the building for?

The honest version of the question is worse. For a very long time, a large part of what schools sold was access. Access to information, rationed through textbooks and lectures. Access to expertise, rationed through credentials. Access to explanation, rationed through the patience of one adult standing in front of thirty children. The whole architecture — the periods, the pacing guides, the curriculum maps — presumes that knowledge is scarce and must be delivered on a schedule, like water from a municipal system.

That presumption died recently. It has not been buried yet, and much of what looks like normal schooling is the refusal to hold the funeral.

I substitute teach. I walk into rooms built on the old presumption, and I watch students live in the new reality. They are not confused about where answers come from. The confusion belongs to the institution, which keeps organizing itself around delivering information to people who are carrying all of it in their pockets. A student who can summon a tireless tutor at midnight is not going to experience a 50-minute lecture as generosity. She is going to experience it as what it increasingly is: a queue.

So what is a teacher for, when answers are free?

Everything that was never actually about answers.

A teacher is for formation — the slow, unglamorous work of turning a person who can find things out into a person who knows what is worth finding out. Judgment is not downloadable, because judgment is not information. It is information that has been lived with, tested, corrected, and carried. A machine can give a student the date of a battle. It cannot care whether she becomes the kind of person who understands why the battle mattered.

A teacher is for sequence — knowing not just what is true but what must come first. Free answers arrive in whatever order you ask for them, and a mind fed in random order grows the way a library shelved at random reads. The curriculum's real product was never the facts. It was the order of the facts, built by people who knew which ideas hold up which other ideas. That craft does not disappear when answers get cheap. It becomes the whole job.

A teacher is for witness. Children learn what adulthood looks like by watching adults work, struggle, pay attention, and tell the truth. A student who watches a teacher wrestle honestly with a hard problem — thinking out loud, getting it wrong, correcting it in front of everyone — has received something no answer engine provides: a demonstration that thinking is a thing a person does, with a character, in public, at some cost to his pride. Education has always been partly apprenticeship in being human. We just used to be able to hide that fact behind the textbook shortage.

And a teacher is for presence in the room — for noticing. The machine does not notice the boy in the third row who has gone quiet this week. It does not notice that the girl who stopped turning in work is not lazy but drowning. Attention, real attention, from a person who knows your name: this was always the scarcest resource in any school, and we built systems that spent it on delivering content instead.

Here is the strange mercy in all of this. The collapse of the information monopoly may finally force schools to become honest about their actual product. The actual product was never the lecture. It was formation, sequence, witness, and attention — the four things that cannot be automated because they are not services. They are relationships.

Catholic education understood this before it had the vocabulary of disruption. The tradition never treated the student as a container to be filled but as a soul to be formed, and it located the authority of the teacher not in his exclusive access to facts but in his responsibility for persons. Sursum corda is not an information policy.

The practical consequences are large, and schools will resist most of them. Fewer lectures, more seminars. Less time delivering, more time discussing, writing, arguing, building, failing in supervised ways. Assessment rebuilt around the question: not can the student produce the answer — the machine settled that — but can the student do anything true and worthwhile with answers she did not have to earn the old way?

The answers are free now. They were, in the deepest sense, always supposed to be. What was never free, and never will be, is a grown person in the room who knows the students, knows the subject, and knows the difference between the two.

That person is not obsolete. That person is finally describable.
